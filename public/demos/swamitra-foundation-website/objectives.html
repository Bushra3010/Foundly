Institutional Purpose

Why a Foundation needs a written mandate

The Objects Clause is the part of the Foundation's constitution that says what it may do and, by implication, what it may not. It was drafted before any programme existed, so that the work would follow the mandate rather than the mandate following the work.

Stated plainly, the Foundation exists to build an India that is Constitutionally Competent, Legally Empowered and Civically Responsible — by advancing constitutional literacy, legal education, civic leadership, institutional excellence, public participation and the Rule of Law through education, research, innovation, capacity building and charitable public service.

Each of the six chapters below opens with the mission it serves and then sets out ten objects. Read together they cover the whole path from constitutional understanding to institutional practice: what people should know, how they should be taught, what evidence should guide it, who it should serve, how the institution should be built, and how it should endure.

Every programme CRF runs, every course it certifies and every framework it publishes is required to sit within one of these sixty objects. Where an idea does not fit, it is not pursued — however popular or well funded it might be.

From Clause to Practice

How the objects become programmes

The Objects Clause is not a formality filed once and forgotten. It is the working test applied to every proposal the Foundation receives.

Before a programme is approved, it must be mapped to a specific object and shown to advance it. Before a research project is commissioned, it must fall within the research and knowledge mission. Before a partnership is signed, the intended activity is checked against the same clause. That mapping is recorded, reviewed by the Board and reported in the Annual Report.

The practical effect is a Foundation that can explain, for any activity it runs, exactly which part of its charitable mandate the activity serves — and can decline work that would take it outside that mandate, whatever the funding attached to it.

A short statement is easy to write and easy to stretch. A detailed clause does the opposite: it commits the Foundation to a defined scope and makes drift visible. It also allows very different activities — a school module, a workplace audit, a survey, a policy brief — to sit under one coherent mandate.
